\section*{Question 12}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Question 12}

Discuss the effect of adding $r_C$ and $r_{leak}$ in the modeling accuracy and simulation numerical efficiency. Can we ignore any element with negligible errors to get a numerically efficient model which is easy to solve?

\subsection*{Analysis}

We tested the effect of $r_{leak}$ and $r_C$ on the system eigenvalues. Results are summarized in \Cref{tab:parasitic_eigenvalues}.

\subsection*{Discussion}

Key insights from the eigenvalue study:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$r_{leak}$ dominates stiffness}: Increasing $r_{leak}$ drives the fastest eigenvalue orders of magnitude larger ($10^7 \to 10^{13}$), directly limiting stable step size. 
    \item \textbf{$r_C$ is negligible}: For fixed $r_{leak}$, varying $r_C$ has almost no effect on eigenvalues or stiffness.
\end{itemize}

From a design perspective:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A \textbf{very small $r_{leak}$} discharges the capacitor quickly but wastes power continuously during normal operation.  
    \item A \textbf{very large $r_{leak}$} minimizes losses but slows down discharge, creating a safety hazard and worsening numerical stiffness.  
    \item In practice, $r_{leak}$ is chosen to guarantee safe discharge (e.g.\ to <50~V in $\sim$1--2 minutes) while keeping continuous losses acceptable.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Conclusion}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Keep $r_{leak}$}: It is essential for a safe discharge path. Its value should be tuned to balance safety (fast enough discharge) against efficiency (not too much continuous loss).
    \item \textbf{Drop $r_C$}: It has negligible effect on accuracy and stiffness; setting $r_C \to \infty$ improves numerical efficiency with no loss of realism.
\end{itemize}

Thus, the optimal strategy is to retain $r_{leak}$ at a practical design value while omitting $r_C$. This yields a numerically simpler model that remains physically meaningful. The system remains stiff regardless, so stiff solvers are still the preferred choice.
